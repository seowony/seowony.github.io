Research Associate Professor

Seo-Won Chang

Research Institute of Basic Sciences, Seoul National University

I'm an observational astronomer working in time-domain and multi-messenger astronomy. I study how the sky changes over minutes to years. Over the past decade I have built the survey infrastructure behind this work, from photometric pipelines and survey databases to real-time transient detection, for SkyMapper, KMTNet and the 7-Dimensional Telescope (7DT).

I led KS4 Data Release 1 (Chang et al. 2026, JKAS), KMTNet's first public survey release, now available through NOIRLab Astro Data Lab and CDS. In 2017 I was part of the discovery of GW170817/AT2017gfo, the first optical counterpart of a gravitational-wave event. I'm also a Visiting Fellow at the Research School of Astronomy and Astrophysics, Australian National University.

Research

Four connected lines

Stellar flares, starspots and how activity changes with age

How magnetic activity in M dwarfs, seen as flares and starspots rotating in and out of view, relates to rotation and age. With large SkyMapper samples I measured the M-dwarf flaring fraction and showed that the link between activity and kinematic age varies across the solar cylinder. I'm now extending this to FGK dwarfs and working towards spectroscopic detection of coronal mass ejections from Sun-like stars.

Blue Large-Amplitude Pulsators beyond the Galactic bulge

BLAPs are hot pulsating stars first found by OGLE in 2017. Nearly 200 are now known, but most lie towards the crowded, reddened Galactic bulge. To form, they must lose most of their outer envelope, yet unlike hot subdwarfs (sdBs) few have binary companions, so how they form is still an open question. I found a new BLAP in SkyMapper, a helium-poor thick-disc star that may point to a different formation channel (Chang et al. 2024, MNRAS). I'm now searching SkyMapper DR4 for more BLAPs outside the bulge, in less crowded and less reddened fields.

Survey infrastructure and AI classification

The tools that make time-domain discovery possible: photometric pipelines, survey databases and public data releases, most recently KS4 Data Release 1. I also work on machine-learning classification of variable stars, periodic variables and real/bogus transients, with the goal of decision-aware AI for the LSST era.

Optical counterparts of gravitational-wave events

I have been searching for the optical light of gravitational-wave events since early 2017, through to the O4 run. I was part of the GW170817/AT2017gfo discovery, built and ran the SkyMapper alert follow-up pipeline through LIGO/Virgo O3 (Chang et al. 2021, PASA), and continue follow-up with KMTNet and 7DT in GECKO. I'm now also turning to the sources themselves: kilonovae from NS–NS and NS–BH mergers, and spotting orphan kilonovae in the Rubin/LSST alert stream from a single-epoch SED.

Projects

Programs I lead with 7DT, 7DS, BOES and Gemini

Measuring periods of new BLAP candidates with 7DT

A search for ultraviolet variability in SkyMapper DR4 has turned up more than a hundred BLAP candidates in southern fields, away from the crowded bulge where almost all known BLAPs have been found. Several 7DT units observe each target at the same time in g and r. Their interleaved exposures give continuous light curves that pin periods down to about 0.1%, as faint as 19th magnitude, in two to three nights of half-hour blocks.

With Christian Wolf, Christopher Onken, Michael Bessell (ANU)
Péter Németh (Czech Academy of Sciences)
7DT core members

Hα activity of southern K and M dwarfs with 7DS

Hα emission is the clearest sign of chromospheric activity in cool stars, but most large samples so far are northern and spectroscopic. 7DS medium-band spectrophotometry (400–900 nm) measures Hα excess across the whole survey footprint. With repeat visits, we can tell steady activity apart from flares on timescales of days to months.

With 7DT core members

Following stellar flares minute by minute

Flares are often modelled as a single ~10,000 K blackbody, but the biggest events are more complicated. As they fade, the blue continuum stays near 8,000–11,000 K while the red end cools to 3,500–5,500 K. Rubin/LSST will catch thousands of these, but in only two filters per visit. This program triggers 7DT within minutes of an alert and follows the full 400–900 nm spectrum, Hα included, as the flare fades.

With 7DT core members

High-resolution spectroscopy of flaring chromospheres

Photometry tells us how much energy a flare releases. Time-resolved high-resolution spectra show what is happening in the chromosphere. With the Bohyunsan Optical Echelle Spectrograph (BOES), we take spectra through individual energetic flares and follow Hα, the Balmer series and other chromospheric lines minute by minute, separately from the continuum. These line profiles are where we look for filament eruptions and the coronal mass ejections that come with them.

Latest Chromospheric line evolution during an energetic flare on AD Leo, Oh et al., JKAS (corresponding author)

How BLAPs form: Gemini spectroscopy and non-LTE models

Pulsation periods alone can't tell the formation channels apart. Binary mass transfer, mergers and supernova survivors each leave a different mark on surface temperature, gravity and abundances, yet only about 15% of the ~200 known BLAPs have measured atmospheric parameters. This NRF Core Research program (2026–2029) combines Gemini spectroscopy with non-LTE model atmospheres (XTgrid / TLUSTY) to build the first homogeneous spectroscopic characterisation of the class.

With Péter Németh (Czech Academy of Sciences & Astroserver.org), XTgrid / TLUSTY modelling

Rubin / LSST

Where my work meets the survey

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time will send out millions of alerts every night. Working with the Fink alert broker, I pick out the events worth chasing and follow them up quickly.

Stellar flares come first. Our Fink filter matches Rubin alerts to K and M dwarfs in real time. When a bright flare goes off, 7DT, which shares the Chilean night with Rubin, can be on the star within minutes. Each of its telescopes observes through a different medium-band filter, so every 100-second frame gives a spectrum-like SED from 400 to 875 nm. Following a flare for an hour lets us track both the hot blue continuum and the cooler red one that grows as the flare fades, and test where that red light comes from.

Kilonovae from neutron star mergers may turn up in the alert stream without a gravitational-wave trigger. A single-epoch SED from 7DT can flag these orphan kilonova candidates (Paek et al. 2026, ApJ).

Fast blue optical transients (FBOTs) brighten and fade within days, so they need to be caught early. I'm building a filter to pull FBOT candidates out of the Fink alert stream.

Very blue stars such as BLAPs may also be within reach. Single-epoch photometry from Rubin DP2 could be enough to pick them out.
